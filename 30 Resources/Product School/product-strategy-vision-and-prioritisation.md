## Module 1: craft an advanced product strategy

**The leadership transition (what changes moving from IC to product lead):** from executing someone else's direction to setting the direction others execute against; from shipping features to making bets on where to play and how to win; from receiving analysis to deciding which questions are worth asking; from owning one area to deciding across teams and workstreams. Feature thinking becomes a strategic compass; your execution becomes other people's clarity.

**Hierarchy:** Vision (stable, why we exist) → Strategy (principles on how we deliver value, differentiate and make money; adapts as we learn) → Roadmap (who builds what, when) → Execution (build, launch, iterate, feed data back up).

**Strategy is not:** a feature/date roadmap, a mission statement, a revenue goal without choices, a write-once document, or a rigid directive. **Strategy is:** a plan for navigating challenges toward the vision, built with deep thought, aspirational enough to align teams, revisited often, and a shared north star that lives outside any one person's doc.

**Three tests for a defensible vision**
| Test | Question | Failure signal |
|---|---|---|
| Market timing | Why now? What shifted in the last 2–3 years? | A vision that would have read the same five years ago |
| Competitive position | Why us? What do we understand that others don't? | Ambition with no real asymmetry |
| Org readiness | Why us, now? What must be true inside to execute? | Big claim, no anchor in current capacity |

**Six-step strategy process:** (1) set the vision; (2) generate insights, meaning a point of view only your team can hold, not raw data; (3) identify challenges honestly; (4) define approaches, where to play and how to win; the hard part is committing to what you will not do; (5) build accountability by cascading to OKRs with owners, kill criteria and review cadence; (6) share early and iterate.

**AI as strategic co-pilot:** synthesise signals you cannot read yourself; stress-test as devil's advocate (write the CFO's rejection memo, argue the competitor's case); monitor competitors continuously. Judgment stays human.

**Frameworks**
- **Playing to Win cascade:** winning aspiration → where to play → how to win → capabilities required → management systems. Rule: capabilities that sound impressive but don't connect back to how you win are noise.
- **Jobs-to-be-done:** use when teams argue features or personas. Key question: what progress is the customer trying to make, functionally, socially, emotionally? (Spotify example: users hire it for the right song for the moment, not to own music.)
- **Platform vs product thinking:** are we building something that wins alone, or the foundation others win on? Platform = integrations completing the feature set (CRM example).
- **Rumelt-style test (diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions):** diagnosis names the specific friction, not "the market is competitive"; a guiding policy creates focus by saying no; actions must reinforce each other. If you can remove one initiative without affecting the others, you have parallel bets, not a strategy. Course case: Figma vs Adobe XD (constraint-driven coherence beat resources without a defining bet).

**OKRs bridge strategy and execution.** Bad KR: a delivery milestone ("ship model by Q2"). Good KR: a measurable result ("race-week retention 34% → 62%").

**Deciding without data (four steps):** (1) name what you know vs assume; (2) split unknowns into resolvable vs live-with; (3) check reversibility, two-way door vs one-way door; (4) make a provisional call and set a trigger: the one signal that would show the bet is wrong. Traps: treating assumptions as facts, paralysis on structural unknowns, applying one-way-door rigour to two-way doors, deciding without naming what would change your mind.

**Six-question self-diagnostic:** clear? makes a hard bet? names the real challenge? do actions cohere? can it cascade to OKRs? is it resourced (strategy without resources is theatre)?

## Module 2: prioritisation and roadmapping

**Rocks, pebbles, sand.** Rocks = strategic bets that move the north star (differentiating; don't let them be starved); pebbles = customer-requested improvements that build trust; sand = maintenance, debt, polish. Choose depth over breadth: leaders who fill sprints with sand and pebbles wonder why rocks never ship. Course examples of rocks: Stripe (API reliability and docs), Notion (block-based editor architecture), Linear (performance and architecture speed).

| Framework | Use when | Mechanics | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Impact vs effort | Fast alignment, 30 min workshop | Plot all items; focus on next sprint/quarter; debate high-impact high-effort against OKRs; rule: double the effort estimate, halve the impact estimate | Quick, coarse |
| RICE | Gut feel is not enough | (Reach × Impact × Confidence) ÷ Effort; confidence 50–100%; effort in person-months | Garbage in, garbage out |
| WSJF | Delay has a measurable cost (SAFe-style environments) | (Time criticality + value + risk reduction) ÷ job size | Easy to game; agree weights first |

Skill = knowing which to reach for when, with one default that is not "the loudest voice in the room". Related: [[10 Projects/AI Second Brain/03_Product_School_Playbook|playbook]] and [[30 Resources/SAFe/artifacts-and-backlogs|SAFe artifacts and backlogs]] (WSJF).

**Roadmap types by audience:** outcome-based (CPO/board), Now/Next/Later (team and stakeholders, signals confidence without dates), timeline (engineering/ops, dependencies), opportunity-based (product/design, jobs and discovery), OKR-linked (anyone needing the work-to-outcome line). Public example: GitHub's public roadmap by quarter, with feature area and release stage.

**Roadmap pitfalls and fixes:** feature factory (write each item as a bet with a linked OKR); hidden roadmap (self-service per stakeholder tier, one source of truth); stale artifact (owner, review tied to sprint cycles); over-promised (add dates only when you'd stake your credibility). Root cause: roadmap became a communication artifact, not a strategic tool.

**Takeaways:** a roadmap is a public statement of what you bet on and in what order, and what is not being built; bets that don't get resourced are intentions, not bets.
